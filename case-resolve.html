Phase 01

Discover - Understanding the problem space

The challenge: Young men experience mental health struggles at alarming rates, yet are significantly less likely to seek professional support. Our team chose to focus on this gap, not just because the statistics demanded it, but because we saw it as a design problem: the tools available weren't built for how men actually engage.
Desktop research grounded the brief in evidence. We then ran a three-stage primary research process: online ethnography on Reddit and social media (observing how men talk about mental health in spaces they feel safe), followed by surveys, then in-depth interviews. Each stage fed into the next.
Stat 01
70% don't seek help
According to the Australian Bureau of Statistics (2022), 70% of men experiencing mental health challenges do not seek professional support.
Stat 02
1 in 5 report loneliness
Movember (2024) found 1 in 5 males report experiencing loneliness, with a steady rise since 2012, isolation compounding help-seeking barriers.
Finding 01
Emotional suppression
67% of men in our survey felt discomfort sharing emotions. Interviews confirmed a deeply felt expectation to "keep calm and find a solution", showing vulnerability felt weak.
Finding 02
Resources feel generic
Participants found existing platforms surface-level. "All existing resources seem so surface-level that no one's actually expressing their emotions", Interview participant.
Finding 03
Need for community
Relatability mattered more than clinical credentials. Men wanted to connect with others who understood their experience, not just access information.
Finding 04
Gap in gamification
Competitor analysis (DailyBean, Rosebud AI, Duolingo) showed no platform combined gamification, personalisation, community, and a focus on young men simultaneously.
Phase 02

Define - Shaping the design problem

We synthesised our research into three core problem areas: distorted perceptions of strength (hiding emotions to appear unaffected), stigma and lack of tailored support (existing resources too generic), and emotional isolation (disconnection intensifying loneliness). We also developed our primary persona, Jordan, 22, a final-year student and gamer who masks his stress and is sceptical of traditional mental health tools.
How might we

"How might we empower young men to combat loneliness and shift their perceptions of emotional vulnerability that encourages them to prioritise their mental well-being?"

Our competitor analysis confirmed the market gap. Duolingo showed us gamification done right; DailyBean showed mood tracking without direction; Rosebud AI showed personalisation without male-focus. Resolve needed to be the intersection of all three, plus community.
P1
Reframe mental health as self-improvement
Use language and metaphors familiar to young men, challenge, progress, resilience, rather than clinical or therapeutic framing.
P2
Personalisation that adapts to the user
Weekly demons generated from mood tracker data, personalised resources, and tasks that reflect each user's specific stressors, not generic content.
P3
Safe community without forced vulnerability
Anonymous daily discussion prompts let men engage with others' experiences and share their own without the pressure of direct emotional disclosure.
Phase 03

Develop - Ideation to prototype

Ideation followed a structured process: Crazy 8s → Decision Matrix → Sketches → Sitemap → Wireflows → Wireframes. Each member generated 8 ideas rapidly; we then scored all top ideas against criteria including personalisation, gamification, community-building, feasibility, and accessibility. The highest-scoring concept, a gamified tracker with weekly "demon battles", was selected and refined via SCAMPER.
The core mechanic: users face a weekly demon tailored to their mood tracker responses (e.g. the Overwhelmancer represents academic/work stress). Daily quests earn Resolve Points (RP). At the end of the week, RP is spent attacking the demon in the Battleground. Defeating it unlocks the next demon. The metaphor externalises the stressor without requiring the user to label their emotion directly.
✦
Wireframes & ideation - Figma View prototype →
Usability testing used a Think Aloud protocol with 4 young men who are gamers, our target audience. Testing was hybrid (in-person and Discord). Participants completed three tasks: journaling via mood tracker, participating in a daily discussion, and tracking progress. A SUS score of 68 was recorded post-testing, with three clear issues surfaced:
Issue 01 → Iteration
Text and buttons too small
Users struggled to read certain text. We increased button and text sizes across the interface, improving tap targets and legibility.
Issue 02 → Iteration
Inconsistent language and icons
Different terminology across screens ("Side Quests" vs "Quests") caused confusion. We standardised language and iconography throughout.
Issue 03 → Iteration
Weak visual hierarchy
Key information was hard to scan. We added stronger section titles, colour blocks, and visual separation to create clearer hierarchy.
My role specifically
Product strategy & sprint facilitation
As PM, I defined the roadmap, ran sprint planning and retrospectives, facilitated critique sessions, and ensured design decisions tied back to research insights.
Phase 04

Deliver - Final prototype and outcomes

The final Resolve prototype covers: onboarding, the home dashboard (demon reveal, daily quests, RP tracker), mood tracker, Wiki (personalised resources + demon info), Quests (daily discussions + personal quests), Battleground, and Profile (achievements, activity summary, mood stats). A Resolve Plus+ subscription tier was also designed, offering 2x RP, exclusive rewards (UberEats, DoorDash vouchers), and an ad-free experience.

What I'd do differently: The SUS score of 68 tells an honest story, we tested too late in the process, which meant the visual hierarchy and icon consistency issues were harder to reverse by the time we found them. I'd push for usability testing at the wireframe stage in any future project, not just at hi-fi. The bigger learning for me was about team accountability. Managing a six-person team across nine weeks with competing schedules and varying levels of commitment was the hardest part of this project, harder than any design decision. I responded to problems as they surfaced rather than building ownership structures from the start. That's something I've actively taken into how I work now, both at DEWR and in running Wellna. A PM who waits for alignment issues to become visible has already lost time they can't get back.
What I learned as PM: Being the person who holds the brief means you're accountable for whether the team solves the right problem, not just whether the prototype looks good. The sharpest moments were when I had to redirect design decisions that were aesthetically strong but diverging from what the research actually said. That tension between creative momentum and research rigour is something I didn't expect to navigate as much as I did, and it's made me a more deliberate collaborator.
