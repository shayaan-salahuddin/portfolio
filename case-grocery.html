Phase 01

Discover - The dietary shopping problem

The pain point: Grocery shopping is a time-consuming and frustrating task for people with specific dietary needs. They spend extra time reading every product label, often unsure whether items are safe for their restrictions, leading to anxiety, wasted time, and sometimes unsafe purchases.
I ran a survey targeting people who actively manage dietary restrictions when grocery shopping. Key dietary requirements represented included halal, dairy-free, high-protein, low-carb, and lactose-free. I also gathered qualitative insights through informal interviews.

"Barcode scanning is essential for me to quickly identify if a product is halal. I don't want to waste time reading every label."

, Survey participant

Finding 01
52% spend significant time label-reading
More than half of surveyed users reported spending significant time reading product labels on every shop, including for familiar products they'd bought before.
Finding 02
Multiple restrictions simultaneously
Most participants managed more than one dietary requirement at once (e.g. halal + high-protein, dairy-free + low-carb). Existing apps handled them one at a time.
Finding 03
Strong interest in barcode scanning
Users consistently asked for a fast way to check product compatibility, barcode scanning emerged as the most requested feature across both survey and interview data.
Finding 04
Preference persistence
"I would definitely use a feature that saves my dietary preferences so I don't have to re-enter them each time." Repetition was a key frustration across existing tools.
Phase 02

Define - Who is this for?

I created a primary persona, Sarah, a busy professional with dietary restrictions who struggles to find suitable products quickly and efficiently. Her goal: get in and out of the supermarket without spending extra time on labels. The problem statement followed directly from the research synthesis.
How might we

"How might we streamline the grocery shopping experience for people with dietary restrictions to help them save time and reduce frustration?"

P1
Dietary filtering as a first-class feature
Filter by dietary requirement (vegan, gluten-free, halal, dairy-free, high-protein) with persistent preferences saved to profile, not a buried setting.
P2
Barcode scanning for instant compatibility check
Users can scan any product in-store and immediately see whether it meets their saved dietary requirements, eliminating label anxiety at the shelf.
P3
In-store usability as a constraint
Large tap targets (min 44px), high contrast, thumb-zone navigation. The interface must work one-handed in motion with a basket in the other hand.
Phase 03

Develop - Wireframes to hi-fi

I moved through three fidelity stages: paper sketches → lo-fi Figma wireframes → hi-fi prototype. At each stage I conducted peer usability testing with classmates who managed dietary requirements, gathering task-completion data and qualitative feedback.
Final Presentation — Canva Open ↗
Iteration 01
Category page was too dense
The initial design showed 6 products per row on mobile. Testing revealed users missed dietary badges at that size. Reduced to 2-column grid with larger product cards.
Iteration 02
Filter UI was hidden
Users in testing didn't find the filter button (top-right corner). Moved to a persistent filter bar below the search field, always visible.
Iteration 03
Dietary badge design
Testing three badge designs, icon only, text only, icon + text, showed icon + text performed best for new users and slowest but most accurate for in-store tasks.
Iteration 04
Recently viewed improved trust
Adding a "Recently Viewed" section to the home screen significantly reduced re-search behaviour, users trusted repeat purchases and could reconfirm quickly.
Phase 04

Deliver - Final design and outcomes

The final hi-fi prototype covers the full shopping journey: onboarding & dietary profile setup, home with personalised recommendations, category browsing with persistent dietary filters, product detail with full ingredient compliance breakdown, and basket/checkout flow.

Reflection: This project sharpened my thinking on information hierarchy, specifically how a single well-placed dietary badge can do more usability work than an entire product detail page. The biggest learning was that filtering UX is often more impactful than the product page itself; if people can't find the right items, the product detail doesn't matter.
